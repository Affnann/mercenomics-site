What he works on

He researches the outsourcing of military and security work to private firms, measuring its costs and how its risks pass to contractors and their workers.

The work is empirical first. Before arguing about this industry, he builds the tables: official contractor counts, injury and death claims, and contract spending, each documented so another person can check it or disagree with it on the evidence.

Why this subject

Two things that are rarely connected. The first is that in recent wars the people doing the work were often not soldiers, and the risk they carried never appeared in the figures the public saw. The second is that many of those people come from countries like his own, recruited into jobs that turned out to be something else. Both are economics problems before they are anything else, and both are measurable.

Research interests

Contracting and incomplete contracts
What armed-service contracts can and cannot specify, and what happens in the gaps they leave.
Principal-agent problems in hired force
Why a force that is paid rather than enlisted behaves differently, and what the buyer gives up in control.
Information asymmetry in recruitment
What workers are told about a job, what the job turns out to be, and who profits from the gap.
Where the cost lands
Who carries the risk of outsourced military work, measured through injury and death claims set against contractor numbers.
