What it is

How large the private security workforce is, and how it compares with the police, is hard to answer because the official figures are scattered across regulators, police agencies and statistics offices, each counting in its own way. This dataset brings the official counts for five countries into one file, with the source file and location of every figure.

In May 2025 the United States had 1,283,470 security guards against 670,520 police and sheriff's patrol officers, or 1.91 guards for every police officer, up from 1.69 in 2014. Japan had 596,985 security guards at the end of 2025, working for 10,949 authorised companies. South Africa had 577,444 security officers registered and recorded as employed in March 2023, out of 2,816,638 registered. In the United Kingdom 460,138 people held an active security licence in January 2026. Australia has had roughly as many guards as police since the early 2000s.

Compare trends, not sizes. Each country counts something different: employees in the US and Australia, guards of authorised companies in Japan, registered officers recorded as employed in South Africa, and licence holders in the UK. Guards per police officer is given only where one source counts both (US and Australia).

What it cannot be used to say

  • The countries count different things, so the size of one country's workforce cannot be set against another's. Compare each country's trend over time.
  • United States and Australia are employment surveys: they count people by occupation, whether they work for a security company or directly for another employer. Australia's quarterly figures move up and down from sampling alone, so the annual table averages the four quarters.
  • United Kingdom figures count licence holders: a licence shows who may legally do licensable security work, not who is working, and some security roles need no licence. The rise to about 2008 reflects licensing being taken up.
  • South Africa counts security officers registered with the regulator and recorded as employed; figures for 2013, 2024 and 2025 are missing.
  • Police are included only where the same source counts both, so that guards and police are measured the same way. For Japan, South Africa and the UK the sources used give no matching police count.

How it was checked

  • Japan: twelve years appear in more than one official publication, and every one agrees.
  • United States: every figure for 2016 to 2023 was read twice, from the Bureau's web tables and its spreadsheets, and all match.
  • Australia: the figures summed from the Bureau's detailed rows match its own summary table for all 80 figures checked.
  • United Kingdom: where the Authority's 2021 and 2026 releases overlap they differ by less than 0.1 percent.
  • South Africa: ten figures appear again in other reports or presentations of the regulator, and all match.
