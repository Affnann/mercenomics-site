How to read these numbers

Each dataset keeps the figures as the official source published them. Nothing has been estimated or filled in, and where a source hides or misprints a number, the file says so rather than correcting it. Every download includes a documentation file with the sources, the definitions, how the numbers were checked, and what they cannot be used to say. Those limits are summarised below each dataset. Read them before drawing a conclusion: most errors in this field come from reading a count as something it is not.

Contractor census archive

For seventeen years the United States Department of Defense counted the contractor personnel working for it in the Central Command area, which covers Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and the surrounding region, and recorded whether they were United States citizens, third-country nationals or local nationals. The series has ended: the April 2025 report states that it is the last.

This archive puts all 68 quarterly reports, from the third quarter of the 2008 financial year to the second quarter of the 2025 financial year, into one checked table. Every table was read twice with two different programs, and both readings agree for all 251 rows.

What it cannot be used to say

  • It counts only contractors on Department of Defense contracts in one command's area. Contractors of other United States agencies are not included, and it is not a count of mercenaries.
  • The areas covered change over time. Afghanistan appears until the end of the 2021 financial year, Iraq is reported together with Syria from 2018, and Israel is included in the totals from late 2021.
  • Three of the original reports contain tables that do not add up. They are kept as printed and flagged, not corrected.

Defense Base Act injury and death claims

The Defense Base Act is a United States law requiring insurance for civilian employees working overseas on United States government contracts. When one of them is injured or killed, a claim is filed, and the Department of Labor counts those claims by country and year. Official casualty figures usually cover only soldiers, so these tables are one of the few public records of harm to civilian contractors.

This dataset brings the yearly tables for the 2009 to 2024 financial years, and the Department's combined table for 2001 to 2024, into one file, with country names made consistent across years.

What it cannot be used to say

  • These are claims, not casualties. The Department of Labor states that the numbers are not official casualty statistics.
  • The year is when the case was opened, not when the injury happened. Afghanistan still has thousands of claims after the 2021 withdrawal.
  • The country is where the injury happened, not the worker's nationality.
  • A blank cell is not zero. The Department leaves small numbers out of its tables, so adding up the countries gives less than the published total.
  • It covers civilian contract work of every kind, including construction, logistics and translation, not only security work.

US federal guard-service contracts

Every United States federal contract action for guard services (product and service code S206) from the 2008 to the 2026 financial year, as reported on USAspending.gov: 215,353 contract actions on 50,726 contracts, for guarding embassies, military bases, immigration detention centres, courthouses and federal buildings. For every year, the cleaned file adds up to USAspending's own published total to the cent.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The amounts are obligations, not payments: money the government commits to pay. Later changes can take money back, so some rows are negative.
  • Amounts are in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation.
  • "Guard" is broad. The code covers armed and unarmed guards, at home and abroad, including detention officers. Most of the money is for work inside the United States.
  • The 2026 financial year is probably incomplete for the Department of Defense, whose contract records arrive with a 90-day delay.

Japan traffic-guard pay rates

Every year Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism sets the daily wage that government cost estimates assume for traffic guards from private security companies on public works, in all 47 prefectures. This dataset turns the ministry's tables for the 2012 to 2026 financial years into one file of 1,410 rows, and adds the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's monthly figures on job openings for each job seeker in security occupations.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The rates are wages, not prices. They leave out the security company's overheads, insurance, training and profit, so they are well below what a client pays per guard-day.
  • They are rates used to estimate public contract costs, not a record of what each guard was actually paid.
  • The job-openings figures cover security occupations in general, not traffic guards alone, and only jobs registered at Japan's public employment offices. They are national, not by prefecture.
  • Special rates after the 2011 earthquake and during COVID-19 are marked in the file.
