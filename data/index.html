How to read these numbers

Each dataset keeps the figures as its source published them. Nothing has been estimated or filled in. Where a figure had to be worked out, such as a date given only as a weekday, the file marks it, and where a source hides or misprints a number, the file says so rather than correcting it. Every download includes a documentation file with the sources, the definitions, how the numbers were checked, and what they cannot be used to say. Those limits are summarised below each dataset. Read them before drawing a conclusion: most errors in this field come from reading a count as something it is not.

Contractor census archive

For seventeen years the United States Department of Defense counted the contractor personnel working for it in the Central Command area, which covers Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and the surrounding region, and recorded whether they were United States citizens, third-country nationals or local nationals. The series has ended: the April 2025 report states that it is the last.

This archive puts all 68 quarterly reports, from the third quarter of the 2008 financial year to the second quarter of the 2025 financial year, into one checked table. Every table was read twice with two different programs, and both readings agree for all 251 rows.

What it cannot be used to say

  • It counts only contractors on Department of Defense contracts in one command's area. Contractors of other United States agencies are not included, and it is not a count of mercenaries.
  • The areas covered change over time. Afghanistan appears until the end of the 2021 financial year, Iraq is reported together with Syria from 2018, and Israel is included in the totals from late 2021.
  • Three of the original reports contain tables that do not add up. They are kept as printed and flagged, not corrected.

Defense Base Act injury and death claims

The Defense Base Act is a United States law requiring insurance for civilian employees working overseas on United States government contracts. When one of them is injured or killed, a claim is filed, and the Department of Labor counts those claims by country and year. Official casualty figures usually cover only soldiers, so these tables are one of the few public records of harm to civilian contractors.

This dataset brings the yearly tables for the 2009 to 2024 financial years, and the Department's combined table for 2001 to 2024, into one file, with country names made consistent across years.

What it cannot be used to say

  • These are claims, not casualties. The Department of Labor states that the numbers are not official casualty statistics.
  • The year is when the case was opened, not when the injury happened. Afghanistan still has thousands of claims after the 2021 withdrawal.
  • The country is where the injury happened, not the worker's nationality.
  • A blank cell is not zero. The Department leaves small numbers out of its tables, so adding up the countries gives less than the published total.
  • It covers civilian contract work of every kind, including construction, logistics and translation, not only security work.

UN peacekeeping contributions

The UN has no army of its own. Every peacekeeper is lent by a member country, and the UN pays that country a fixed monthly amount for each soldier or police officer in a formed unit, which is why peacekeeping is sometimes described as a market in which governments supply security for a price. This series joins the UN's two monthly files into one checked record of 429 months and 162 contributing countries, from November 1990 to July 2026, with Bangladesh's place over time, the 2025-26 cut, and an estimate of what the UN owes countries for their troops.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The full UN dataset is not in the download. The UN's terms forbid redistributing its data, so the download holds summary tables, charts and the code that rebuilds the full dataset from the UN's own files.
  • The reimbursement estimate is money owed at the standard rate, not cash paid. It leaves out equipment payments, bonuses and penalties, and the UN often pays late.
  • The totals are UN uniformed personnel in every mission in the files, including a few political missions and support offices, as the UN itself reports them.
  • Before May 2017, staff officers are counted inside troops, so reimbursement estimates for those years are slightly overstated.

Private security workforce by country

How many private security guards the United States, Japan, South Africa, the United Kingdom and Australia have, year by year, from each government's own records, and, where the same source also counts police, how many guards there are for every police officer. In May 2025 the United States had 1,283,470 security guards against 670,520 police and sheriff's patrol officers: 1.91 guards for every police officer, up from 1.69 in 2014.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The countries count different things (employees, licence holders, registered officers), so compare each country's trend, not one country's size against another's.
  • Guards per police officer is given only for the United States and Australia, where one survey counts both.
  • UK figures count people holding a security licence, not people working; Australia's figures are survey estimates.

Enforcement actions against guard contractors

Federal enforcement actions against the 48 largest U.S. government guard contractors, hand-coded from Department of Justice and Equal Employment Opportunity Commission press releases, with the source link, the amount in the release's own words and what the release says about liability. 48 actions from 2009 to 2025, 34 of them resolved, including Paragon Systems' $52 million False Claims Act settlement (2024); none of the companies is on the SAM.gov list of current federal exclusions.

What it cannot be used to say

  • Most settlements are not findings of wrongdoing: many state the claims are allegations only, and lawsuits marked as filed were allegations when announced.
  • It cannot rank companies by misconduct: bigger contractors face more cases, and agencies choose which to announce.
  • A company with no rows does not have a clean record; press releases are not the full enforcement record, and years before 2013 are thin.

US federal guard-service contracts

Every United States federal contract action for guard services (product and service code S206) from the 2008 to the 2026 financial year, as reported on USAspending.gov: 215,353 contract actions on 50,726 contracts, for guarding embassies, military bases, immigration detention centres, courthouses and federal buildings. For every year, the cleaned file adds up to USAspending's own published total to the cent.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The amounts are obligations, not payments: money the government commits to pay. Later changes can take money back, so some rows are negative.
  • Amounts are in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation.
  • "Guard" is broad. The code covers armed and unarmed guards, at home and abroad, including detention officers. Most of the money is for work inside the United States.
  • The 2026 financial year is probably incomplete for the Department of Defense, whose contract records arrive with a 90-day delay.

Japan traffic-guard pay rates

Every year Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism sets the daily wage that government cost estimates assume for traffic guards from private security companies on public works, in all 47 prefectures. This dataset turns the ministry's tables for the 2012 to 2026 financial years into one file of 1,410 rows, and adds the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's monthly figures on job openings for each job seeker in security occupations.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The rates are wages, not prices. They leave out the security company's overheads, insurance, training and profit, so they are well below what a client pays per guard-day.
  • They are rates used to estimate public contract costs, not a record of what each guard was actually paid.
  • The job-openings figures cover security occupations in general, not traffic guards alone, and only jobs registered at Japan's public employment offices. They are national, not by prefecture.
  • Special rates after the 2011 earthquake and during COVID-19 are marked in the file.

Bangladesh migration costs

Bangladesh sets a legal maximum on what a worker may be charged to migrate for a job abroad. This dataset sets what workers actually paid, from the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics' Cost of Migration Survey 2020, against those caps. Workers who left between 2015 and 2018 paid 2 to 4 times the cap on average, depending on the destination, and 77.6 percent borrowed to go. It comes with a calculator that shows one worker how long it takes to earn the cost back.

What it cannot be used to say

  • The caps come from news reports. The government notices setting them could not be found online, and caps may have changed since.
  • These are averages, not individuals. The survey reports averages only, for six destinations.
  • Survey workers left in 2015 to 2018, while the caps used were revised in late 2017, so some workers left under earlier caps.
  • It is about labour migration in general, not only security work.

Sundarbans kidnapping for ransom

Armed groups in the Bangladesh Sundarbans abduct fishers, crab collectors, honey gatherers and woodcutters and hold them until their families pay, and charge them a fee per boat to enter the forest at all. No official dataset of these incidents exists. This dataset codes 63 reported incidents involving 439 people, February 2015 to October 2026, from Bangla and English news reports, with reported ransoms and 9 records of protection payments, so the size, prices and timing of this protection market can be measured.

What it cannot be used to say

  • It counts reported incidents only. Many abductions are never reported, so totals are a lower bound.
  • From 2025 many reports come from Coast Guard press releases, so part of the recent rise may be more reporting, not only more abductions.
  • Ransom amounts come from press reports, often from relatives, and are not verified.
  • No names of victims, families or accused individuals are published, and no article text.
